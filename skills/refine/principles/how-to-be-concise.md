# How to be concise

**Source**: Wes Kao, "How to be concise"

## The core idea

Concision isn't about being short. It's about being **information-dense**: saying more per word. A 200-word note can be more concise than a 50-word one if the 50 words are filler.

Kao's lens: remove anything that isn't earning its place. Every sentence should either move the reader toward the point or be deliberately softening (e.g., warmth signals in a peer Slack). If it's neither, cut it.

## Why it matters

Busy readers skim. The more words you use to say something, the more you teach the reader to skip. Worse, wordiness signals junior thinking: it looks like you haven't figured out the point yet.

Concision also forces you to decide. You can't be vague in 15 words.

## Concrete tactics (drawn from Kao's tactics and adjacent advice)

### 1. Cut the throat-clearing
Words that signal "I'm about to say something" without saying it.
- *"I wanted to reach out to…"* → cut
- *"Just a quick thought…"* → cut
- *"Hope this finds you well…"* → cut (unless writing to a customer cold)
- *"I was wondering if maybe…"* → cut; replace with the direct question

### 2. Cut the hedges
Hedges dilute authority. Sometimes one hedge is fine; three in a sentence is always too many.
- *"I think maybe we could perhaps consider…"* → "I recommend…"
- *"It might be kind of a good idea to possibly…"* → "Let's…"
- *"I'm not totally sure but it seems like…"* → "My read:"

Exception: when you genuinely are uncertain, one calibrated hedge is honest. *"I think"* is fine. *"I think maybe"* is not.

### 3. Cut redundancy
- *"The end result"* → "the result"
- *"Basic fundamentals"* → "fundamentals"
- *"In order to"* → "to"
- *"At this point in time"* → "now"
- *"Due to the fact that"* → "because"

### 4. Prefer verbs to noun-phrases
- *"Make a decision about"* → "decide"
- *"Have a conversation regarding"* → "discuss"
- *"Do an analysis of"* → "analyse"
- *"Take into consideration"* → "consider"

### 5. Cut the meta
Commentary about the writing itself is almost always trim-able.
- *"As I mentioned above…"* → just say it
- *"I'll keep this brief…"* → be brief, don't announce it
- *"This is a bit of a ramble but…"* → cut and un-ramble

### 6. Combine sentences that share a subject
Two short sentences with the same subject often collapse into one with a comma.
- *"The launch is on track. It's scheduled for June 3rd."* → "The launch is on track for June 3rd."

### 7. Cut examples that don't add information
One well-chosen example beats three mediocre ones. If example 2 and 3 say the same thing as example 1, delete them.

### 8. Lead with the verb of the ask
- *"If you could at some point let me know your thoughts on this, that would be helpful"* → "Thoughts by Thursday?"

### 9. Replace "I feel like" with "I think" or just the statement
- *"I feel like we should"* → "We should" or "I recommend"

### 10. The three-minute delete pass (word-level)

From Kao's "Take 3 minutes to delete these words". Run this as the final pass on any draft: each of these words is either doing nothing or doing damage.

| Delete | Why | Replace with |
|---|---|---|
| "fine" | Sounds like you're not fine | "good", "sounds good", "I'm on board" |
| "however" | Bureaucratic, melodramatic "but" | "but", or restructure |
| "unfortunately" | Makes neutral news sound catastrophic; implicitly accepts blame | State it plainly, or "but" |
| "honestly" / "to be honest" | Implies the rest wasn't | Just say the thing |
| "just" ("just wanted to…") | Shrinks the sentence and the sender | Delete it |
| "truly / very / really" (stacked) | Melodrama by intensifier | One stronger word, or none |
| "no problem" | Diminishes effort you actually spent | "glad it helped", "my pleasure" |
| double negatives ("not wrong", "nothing but positive") | Two mental reversals | The direct positive ("has a point", "all positive") |
| "can't until X" | Cedes power; states the closed door | What you CAN do ("I can do Thursday") — see `principles/speak-in-the-affirmative.md` |

## The concision edit pass

After writing the draft, do one pass where the only goal is to cut 20% of the words without losing meaning. You'll almost always find it. If you can't, you were probably already lean.

For higher-stakes pieces: cut 30%.

## Before / after

**Before (149 words):**
> Hi team, I wanted to reach out to share some thoughts on what I've been thinking about regarding the Q2 roadmap. As you probably know, we've been kind of going back and forth on whether to prioritize the onboarding redesign or the billing migration, and I think maybe it makes sense to do the onboarding first, although I can see arguments for both. Some of the reasons I'm leaning this way are: the data we've been seeing on activation isn't great, and also the billing migration is a bigger lift that we probably won't be able to finish this quarter anyway, so maybe it's better to start it in Q3 when we can do it properly. Let me know what you think and we can chat about it further if you want.

**After (55 words):**
> On Q2 priorities: recommending onboarding redesign over billing migration.
>
> Two reasons: activation data is weak and needs attention now, and billing is a bigger lift than fits in Q2 anyway (better done properly in Q3).
>
> Objections welcome. Lock-in by Friday?

Same substance. 63% fewer words. Easier to act on.

## When NOT to apply aggressively

- **Warm peer messages**: a little filler is friendship, not bloat.
- **Customer de-escalation**: a cold, minimal message in a tense moment reads as dismissive.
- **Cross-cultural comms**: directness norms vary; what's concise in one register is brusque in another.

Even in these cases, cut the filler that's doing no work. Just don't sand off the warmth.

## Common failure mode

Cutting so hard that the message becomes cryptic. Concision means high information per word, not low word count at all costs. "Onboarding" is not a concise message; "Recommending onboarding over billing: data's weak, billing won't fit in Q2" is.
