# Speak in the affirmative

**Source**: Wes Kao, "Speak in the affirmative"

## The core idea

"Do this" is easier to process than "don't do that." Negative phrasing forces the reader to mentally reverse it: first picture the thing, then negate it, then work out what you actually want instead. Affirmative phrasing hands them the destination directly.

This applies to three common shapes:

1. **Instructions and feedback**: say what to do, not what to avoid.
2. **Availability and capability**: say what you *can* do, not what you can't.
3. **Decisions and boundaries**: state the path chosen, not the paths rejected.

## Why it matters

Negative phrasing has two costs. The obvious one is cognitive load: the reader does extra work to invert it. The subtle one is that stating the negative *plants the negative*. "Don't worry about the timeline" makes the reader worry about the timeline. "I can't meet before 11" reads as an obstacle; "I can meet from 11" reads as an opening. Same facts, different residue.

It also quietly cedes power. "I can't until Thursday" frames you as constrained. "I can do Thursday" frames you as available. Especially in customer comms and managing up, the affirmative version keeps you in the driver's seat without changing a single fact.

## How to apply it

### 1. Flip instructions to the target behaviour
- 🚫 "Don't spend the whole standup on blockers."
- ✅ "Keep blockers to 2 minutes, then move to priorities."

### 2. State what you CAN do
- 🚫 "I can't look at this until after the board prep."
- ✅ "I can pick this up Thursday, straight after board prep."

### 3. If you must state the negative, fill the void
Sometimes the negative is the message (a boundary, a correction). Fine — but never leave a vacuum. Always follow with the affirmative replacement, or the reader is left knowing what's wrong and guessing at what's right.
- 🚫 "Don't ship without QA sign-off."
- ✅ "Don't ship without QA sign-off. Get QA's tick in the release channel first, then ship."

### 4. Watch for stacked negation
Double negatives ("not wrong", "not unreasonable", "nothing but positive") force two reversals. State the positive directly: "has a point", "reasonable", "all positive".

## Before / after

**Before:**
> Just flagging, we can't get the export feature into this sprint, and I don't want anyone promising it to customers until we've scoped it. Don't bring it up in the customer call.

**After:**
> Export feature lands next sprint at the earliest; I'll confirm scope by Friday. For the customer call, keep the focus on the RFI dashboard, and if export comes up, say it's being scoped with a date to follow.

Same constraints. The second version tells everyone what to *do*, so nobody has to derive it.

## Common failure mode

Toothless positivity: flipping to the affirmative but losing the constraint. "Ship whenever you're ready!" is affirmative and wrong if QA sign-off is mandatory. The affirmative version must carry the same information as the negative one — it changes the framing, never the facts.

## Pairs with

The "I can't until X" pattern shows up most in scheduling replies and cross-functional asks. Default rewrite: state the earliest true "can" and stop. In customer-facing replies this pairs directly with finesse (`principles/finesse.md`): "we don't support X yet" becomes "X is on the roadmap; here's what handles that today."
