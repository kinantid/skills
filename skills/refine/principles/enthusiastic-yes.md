# How to get an enthusiastic yes

**Source**: Wes Kao, "How to get an enthusiastic yes"

## The core idea

There's a big difference between a "yes, I guess" and a "yes, absolutely." The first creates reluctant compliance; the second creates momentum, advocacy, and follow-through.

To get an enthusiastic yes, you have to make saying yes feel obvious (even slightly exciting) to the person you're asking.

## Why it matters

A tepid yes is fragile. The person may not prioritise the work. They may quietly downgrade the effort. They may not defend your idea when someone else questions it. An enthusiastic yes is durable: the person will actively push the thing forward without you having to chase.

This matters especially for:

- Asks to leaders (CEO, execs) for headcount, budget, prioritisation
- Asks to cross-functional partners for eng/design/marketing support
- Customer "yes" on renewals, upsells, or pilot programs

## How to apply it

Before making the ask, run through these:

### 1. Lead with the benefit to *them*, not to you
*"I need eng help on X"* is about you. *"This'll save the eng team the weekly on-call burn from Y issue"* is about them. When possible, frame the ask as something that solves their problem too.

### 2. Pre-empt the work (the MOO for effort)
Enthusiastic yesses come when the person doesn't have to think about what it would take. If you've already scoped the work, named the risks, and proposed the path, the yes is just approval, not a new project.

### 3. Make the ask specific and bounded
- Vague: *"Could we work together on this?"*
- Specific: *"Can I book 30 min with you Thursday to sign off on the pricing deck?"*

Specific asks are easier to say yes to than vague ones.

### 4. Remove friction from the yes
- Pre-draft the Slack / email they'd send to approve.
- Pre-book a calendar slot, they accept or move it.
- Pre-identify who else needs to be looped in, so they don't have to.

### 5. Show momentum
People say yes more enthusiastically to things that are already moving. *"[eng lead] and [data lead] are both in, I just need you as the third."* Social proof + nearly-done framing.

### 6. Make the cost of no visible (gently)
If there's a real consequence to saying no, name it, not as a threat, but as information. *"If we don't move on this by Friday we lose the launch window."*

### 7. Calibrate the size of the ask
A tiny ask gets a fast yes. If what you want is too big for an enthusiastic yes, break it into a smaller first step. *"Just sign off on the direction, we can debate the details after."*

### 8. Replace vague urgency with concrete time
"ASAP", "urgent", "time-sensitive", and "priority" have no fixed meaning: the reader can't tell "next hour" from "this quarter", so they either panic or ignore it. Every vague-urgency word in an ask is a yes-killer, because the recipient can't plan the yes. Swap in one of:

- **A concrete deadline**: "by Thursday EOD", not "soon".
- **A sequence**: "do X first, then Y; Z can wait until next sprint."
- **An anti-deadline**: "this one can genuinely wait until next month" (telling people what's *not* urgent buys credibility for when something is).
- **A default**: "if I don't hear back by 5pm, I'll run with option A." Only for lower-risk, already-aligned decisions, but it converts silence into progress.

## Before / after

**Before (tepid-yes-inviting):**
> Hey [CEO], I was thinking it might be worth doing some research into usage patterns so we can maybe figure out if there's a better pricing model for the future. It might take a few weeks and would involve eng and data. Let me know what you think.

**After (enthusiastic-yes-inviting):**
> [CEO], two-week pricing experiment that could lift ARR by 5% without re-signing customers. I've scoped it with [eng lead] and [data lead], we can run it in parallel to Q2 work, no new headcount. Want me to kick off Monday?

The second version: leads with outcome for the CEO ("5% ARR lift"), pre-scopes the work, shows momentum ("I've scoped it with [eng lead] and [data lead]"), and ends with a specific, bounded action ("kick off Monday?"). Easy yes.

## The "would I say yes to this?" test

Before sending, read it as if you were the recipient. Would you say yes enthusiastically? Would you say yes reluctantly? Would you want to push back?

If the honest answer is "reluctantly", you need to rework the ask: either make it smaller, pre-scope more, or reframe the benefit. Don't send until the honest answer is "yes, and I'd actually find this useful."

## Common failure mode

Burying the ask. If the person has to read four paragraphs to figure out what you want from them, they're already annoyed before they decide. Lead with the ask, justify it briefly, close with the action.

## Special case: the "no" you're actually fine with

Sometimes you're asking for approval on something you don't strongly need. In that case, an enthusiastic "no" is also a good outcome: it saves time. Make the ask easy to decline: *"Low confidence on this one: if you'd rather not, no pushback from me. Just flagging it as an option."* This preserves the relationship and makes the person more likely to say enthusiastic yes *next* time.
