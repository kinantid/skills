# Managing Up

**Source**: Wes Kao, "15 principles for managing up"

## The core idea

Managing up isn't sucking up. It's making your manager's (or any senior stakeholder's) job easier so they can make yours easier in return. Done well, it buys you autonomy, trust, and the best projects. Done poorly (or not at all), it caps your career.

## The principles

Adapted and reworded for a written-comms context — not a reproduction of Kao's article. For her full set in her own words, read the original: [Wes Kao, "15 principles for managing up"](https://www.weskao.com/).

### 1. Make their job easier
Every interaction should lower their cognitive load or unblock something. If you're adding work to their plate, compensate with disproportionate value.

### 2. Anticipate what they'll need before they ask
If they're about to brief the board, send them the metric they'll want. If a launch is coming, send the one-pager before they ask. This is the difference between reacting and leading.

### 3. Bring solutions, not just problems
When flagging an issue, bring your recommendation. *"X is broken. I think we should do Y; here's why; want me to run with that?"* Not: *"X is broken, what do you want to do?"*

### 4. Summarise up, detail down
Senior people want the headline and the ask. Peers and juniors can handle the full context. Calibrate for who's reading.

### 5. Don't surprise them
Bad news travels fast; you should travel faster. Never let your manager learn about a problem from someone else. Flag early, even when it's uncertain.

### 6. State your recommendation, then the reasoning
BLUF: bottom line up front. *"Recommending we push the launch. Reasoning below."* Not: *"Here are five considerations, let's see where we land."*

### 7. Write like you'd want to read
Short sentences. Clear asks. Signposts. No jargon unless necessary.

### 8. Match their communication style
If they're terse, be terse. If they use bullets, use bullets. Don't force them to translate your style to theirs.

### 9. Make asks specific
*"Can you approve X by Thursday?"* not *"Let me know your thoughts when you get a chance."*

### 10. Give them optionality
When the decision is theirs, present 2–3 options with your recommendation flagged. Don't rob them of the choice; just narrow it down.

### 11. Close the loop
When they ask for something, confirm receipt, deliver, confirm done. Silence reads as dropped.

### 12. Don't over-apologise
Senior people hate apology inflation. Apologise once, fix it, move on. *"Missed the deadline. Here's what I'm doing to fix it."* Not five paragraphs of remorse.

### 13. Push back on substance, not on style
When you disagree, be direct but framed around the work. *"I don't think this will land with customers because X. Can we revisit?"* Not: *"I just don't feel good about this."*

### 14. Own the read
If your manager says *"I'm confused by this,"* it's your writing that failed, not their reading. Assume the burden of clarity.

### 15. Protect their time
Don't book 30 min when you need 5. Don't Slack eight messages when one would do. Don't ask questions you can google. Their calendar is a resource; you're a steward of it.

## How this skill uses the principles

When refining a message going **upward** (CEO, manager, exec, board), these principles apply strictly. In particular:

- Principle 1 (make their job easier) → flag the decision you want, don't leave it ambiguous.
- Principle 3 (solutions not problems) → if flagging an issue, include your recommendation.
- Principle 6 (BLUF) → always.
- Principle 9 (specific asks) → always.
- Principle 12 (don't over-apologise) → strip "sorry to bother", "just wanted to flag", "if you have a sec".
- Principle 15 (protect their time) → strictest possible concision pass.

## Before / after

**Before (bad managing up):**
> Hi [CEO], hope your week is going well! I'm really sorry to bother you but I was wondering if you might have some time to talk about the Q3 roadmap. I know you're super busy so no rush at all, just whenever works! I have some thoughts I wanted to share but I'm not sure what the best approach is. Let me know what you think when you get a sec. Thanks so much!!

Violates: 7 (style), 9 (specific), 11 (close loop unclear), 12 (over-apologise), 15 (protect time).

**After:**
> [CEO], want to lock Q3 roadmap this week. Recommending onboarding revamp as P0 (memo attached, 2-min read). 15 min Wed or Thu to sign off?

Applies: 1, 3, 6, 9, 15. One clear ask, one clear recommendation, one clear time box.

## Common failure mode

Confusing managing up with performing. If you're sending constant updates about how hard you're working, that's not managing up. That's optics. Managing up is invisible when it works; the manager just notices their life got easier.
