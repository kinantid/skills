# Invert the but

**Source**: Wes Kao, "Use 'but' strategically" (Parts I & II)

## The core idea

"But" negates whatever comes before it. "[Positive], but [negative]" cancels the positive in the reader's mind: the compliment evaporates and only the criticism sticks. When both halves are true and you want the encouraging note to survive, invert the order: "[negative], but [positive]."

- 🚫 "The deck looks great, but the pricing slide needs a rethink."
- ✅ "The pricing slide needs a rethink, but overall the deck looks great."

Same two facts. The first reads as criticism with a soft opener; the second reads as encouragement with a clear fix.

## Why it matters

Readers weight the end of a sentence. Whatever follows the "but" is what they carry away, quote, and remember. Most drafts order positive-then-negative out of politeness instinct, which accidentally guarantees the negative wins. Ordering is a free lever: it changes what lands without changing what's true.

## How to apply it

1. **Find every "but" (and "however", "that said")** in the draft.
2. **Check what follows it.** Is that the note you want ringing in the reader's ear? If yes, leave it. If the encouraging half is being cancelled, invert.
3. **Decide which note should win on purpose.** Sometimes the negative *should* win: a serious risk flag to the CEO should not end on "but overall we're fine." Inversion is a tool for when the positive is the honest headline, not a rule that every sentence must end happy.

## The guardrails (where inversion goes wrong)

- **Never fabricate the positive.** Inversion only works when both halves are genuinely true. If there's nothing good to say about the work, don't invent it; the honest positive might be about the process ("glad you brought this to me early").
- **Don't build a sandwich.** The positive-after-but is a *closer*, not padding around buried feedback. Keep the negative half specific and actionable in the same breath; don't smother it in praise until it disappears.
- **Don't invert risk flags.** When the reader needs to act on the negative (incidents, slipped deadlines, budget overruns), the negative is the headline. See `principles/objective-not-detached.md`.

## Before / after

**Before (feedback to a designer):**
> The onboarding flow is really strong and the empty states are a big improvement, but the permissions screen doesn't match the patterns we agreed on and honestly it undoes a lot of the good work.

**After:**
> The permissions screen doesn't match the patterns we agreed (it needs the same card layout as steps 1–3), but the rest of the flow is strong, and the empty states are a big improvement.

The fix is now first and specific; the encouragement closes and survives.

## Common failure mode

Mechanical inversion of every sentence until the writing sounds like a verbal tic. Use it at the moments that decide the message's emotional residue: the opening line, the feedback line, the close. One well-placed inversion beats five reflexive ones.

## Pairs with

Shows up most in design/eng feedback and in customer replies that pair a limitation with a capability. In customer copy it composes with finesse: state the constraint once, then close on the capability ("Offline mode is later this year, but everything syncs the moment you're back in coverage, and nothing is lost in between").
