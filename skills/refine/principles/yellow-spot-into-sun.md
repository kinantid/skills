# Turn a Yellow Spot into the Sun

**Source**: Wes Kao, "Turning a yellow spot into the sun"

## The core idea

When you have a real win, a real insight, or a real differentiator, don't mention it casually in the middle of a paragraph. Give it the weight it deserves. Turn the yellow spot into the sun.

Most people underplay their wins, out of modesty, out of fear of seeming self-promotional, or just out of bad drafting habits. The result: the reader glides past the thing that should have landed hardest.

## Why it matters

If you've done good work and don't signal its significance, the reader won't do the interpretive work for you. They'll assume the work was routine. Over time this means your good work goes uncredited and your bad work gets noticed more than it should.

In customer / marketing copy, the stakes are higher: a value prop buried in a paragraph is a value prop no one sees. And competitors who *do* spotlight their equivalent wins will outshine you, even if their work is objectively worse.

## How to apply it

Ask, for each piece of communication: **what's the most important thing in here?** Then structurally elevate it.

Tactics:

### 1. Move it to first position
The first sentence is where attention is highest. Put the sun there.

### 2. Give it its own sentence
A win in a sub-clause is a win wasted. *"We also saw activation improve."* → *"Activation improved 34%."*

### 3. Use specific numbers
Specificity is weight. *"Big improvement"* is dim. *"34% lift, up from 12% the prior quarter"* is bright.

### 4. Name the significance
Don't just state the fact; name why it matters. *"Activation hit 41%: first time above target in 6 quarters."* The second clause does the work.

### 5. Use formatting judiciously
Bold, headers, or isolation (a line break on each side) can spotlight without being gaudy.

### 6. Don't over-hedge it
*"I think maybe this might suggest…"* is a sun-dimmer. If the data is solid, state it solidly.

## When NOT to apply

If a "win" is fragile, preliminary, or context-dependent, spotlighting it is a credibility risk. You'll look foolish if the number moves. Calibrate the spotlight to your confidence level.

And don't spotlight *every* sentence. If everything is the sun, nothing is. One sun per message, maybe two.

## Before / after

**Before (sun buried):**
> This quarter had some ups and downs. The onboarding revamp went live and things have been going well there, with some improvement in activation. We also shipped the billing migration which took longer than expected. Retention was roughly flat. Customer NPS went up a bit.

The actual wins (activation improvement, NPS lift) are footnotes.

**After (sun centred):**
> **Activation hit 41% this quarter, our highest ever, and up from 28% at the start of the year.** That's driven primarily by the onboarding revamp.
>
> Also worth noting: NPS up 6 points. Retention flat. Billing migration shipped (late, but shipped).

Same facts. The sun is now the sun. The other things are secondary notes. A reader skimming the top line gets the story.

## Customer / marketing application

Yellow-spot-into-sun is especially important in marketing copy. Your strongest proof point should be the first thing a reader sees, not the third paragraph.

**Before:**
> [product] helps teams manage projects more efficiently. We offer a range of features including scheduling, document management, and reporting. Our customers include [customer A] and [customer B], and many have reported significant time savings.

**After:**
> [customer] cut project admin time by 40% in their first quarter on [product].
>
> [product] is the project management platform built specifically for teams like yours: scheduling, docs, reporting, all in one place.

The second version leads with the sun (concrete result from a recognisable customer), then fills in the supporting story.

## Applying this

Most PMs tend to underplay their own wins in updates. When refining status updates, actively look for buried wins and elevate them. Don't invent. Only spotlight real things. But make sure real things get their due.

## Common failure mode

Confusing "elevate" with "exaggerate." Yellow-spot-into-sun isn't about overstating. It's about giving real wins the structural prominence they deserve. If you have to inflate the facts to make them sound impressive, the problem isn't the spotlight; it's the substance.

## The test

After writing, read just the first sentence (or the bolded headers, if there are any). Does the reader get the most important thing? If no, restructure. If the sun is hiding somewhere in the third paragraph, move it.
