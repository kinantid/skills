# Finesse

**Source**: Wes Kao, "The unspoken skill of finesse"

## The core idea

Finesse is good judgment applied to delicate scenarios, almost always involving communicating with other humans. It's the skill of getting the outcome you want in situations that have grey area: where the facts are fixed but how you frame them, what you choose to say, and how you say it all move the result.

The key move: **same facts, different framing, different outcome.** Finesse is not lying and it's not spin. It's choosing which true things to lead with, and wording them so the reader interprets accurate information in the most favourable light you can honestly support.

## Why it matters

Two people can answer the same question with the same underlying facts and produce completely different impressions. One leaves the reader reassured and ready to act; the other leaves them uncertain, hunting for the catch. The difference isn't honesty. It's finesse.

This is the layer that sits *on top* of the other principles. BLUF tells you to lead with the point; finesse tells you which true point to lead with. Concise tells you to cut; finesse tells you what to cut so the remainder reads well. It's the judgment that decides how all the other principles get applied to a specific person in a specific moment.

## The six steps (what finesse actually is, unpacked)

Finesse looks like instinct but it's a sequence you can run deliberately:

1. **Read the situation.** What's actually going on, including what's unsaid?
2. **Know your levers.** What can you offer, frame, concede, or withhold?
3. **Identify the risks.** How could this be misread? What's the downside?
4. **Decide what to communicate.** Which true things, in which order?
5. **Decide how to communicate it.** Tone, framing, word choice, channel.
6. **Execute.** Actually say it, and adjust live.

Most people skip 1-3 and jump to 4. The finesse is in the front half.

## The worked example (the heart of this principle)

A customer asks: *"How many users can your platform serve at any one time?"* Same fact in every answer: roughly 500.

**Person A (matter-of-fact, misses the chance):**
> 500 users.

True, but inert. No framing, so the customer supplies their own, and "500" with no context can read as a ceiling or a worry.

**Person B (adds context, but in the negative):**
> We don't have hard limits, and most customers run without major issues. You might hit some performance issues at scale, but it's usually fine.

This is the trap. Every clause plants a doubt: *"don't have hard limits," "without major issues," "performance issues," "but," "usually."* The reader walks away less confident than before they asked. Negative framing of true facts is worse than no framing.

**Person C (same facts, honest positive frame):**
> The platform comfortably handles 500 concurrent users, which covers teams well beyond your current size. As you grow, it scales with you, and we'll flag well ahead of any threshold so it's never a surprise.

Identical information. Person C just led with the capability, stated the headroom, and turned "what happens at scale" from a worry into a managed promise. No claim here is false. That's the bar: **frame for confidence without crossing into marketing-speak or anything you can't back.**

## Find the lever before you touch the words

The six steps run front to back for a reason. A negative-language pass on its own swaps words, and the reader sees a nicer-sounding version of the same answer. The real move comes from step 2: look for a true lever that changes the shape of the answer, then build the message around it.

- **The weak fact:** "you'd be our first customer on this integration".
- **Words-only rewrite:** "you'd be our first customer, which is why the sandbox matters". It's softer, but the reader's worry (untested code near their books) is still unanswered.
- **Lever-led rewrite:** the product already let the customer connect with export switched off, then switch it on themselves. So the answer became a staged rollout with their sign-off at each step (test copy → read-only → writes on). "You'd be first" now reads as the reason the plan exists, and the reader gets the control they were asking for.

To find a lever, ask what the reader is actually afraid of losing (control, money, time, face). Then check the product, the contract or the process for a true option that hands it back to them. Confirm the lever exists before you offer it. A lever you can't deliver is spin.

## How to apply it

When the stakes are delicate (a customer question, a CEO ask, a no you have to deliver), run a two-pass edit:

1. **Negative-language audit.** Scan the draft for words that plant doubt: *don't, without, issues, problems, but, usually, should be fine, no major, hopefully.* Each one is a place where you stated a true thing in a way that costs you. Rewrite to the positive truth.
2. **Misinterpretation pass.** Read it as the most skeptical version of your reader. Where could they read a ceiling, a hedge, or a catch? Close that gap before you send.

Then sanity-check against the bar: every reframe must still be true and defensible. If you can't back it, it's spin, not finesse.

## Plausible linkage (answer the question you want to answer)

Marc Randolph's observation: a skilled operator can "answer whichever question you want to answer" by establishing **plausible linkage** between what was asked and the more useful thing you'd rather address. This is a more aggressive cousin of the question-behind-the-question move: not just decoding the real question, but bridging from the literal question to the answer that serves the outcome. Requires judgment about the relationship; overused, it reads as dodging. See `principles/qbq.md`.

## Personality-message-delivery fit

The same sentence that's authentic from one person is off-key from another. A cheeky, confident line lands from someone whose voice carries it and clangs from someone it doesn't fit. Finesse includes knowing your own register: don't borrow framing that you can't deliver convincingly. Match the move to the messenger.

## Always have a goal

Underneath all of it: know what you want the audience to *do* before you open your mouth. If you're not sure, figure it out first, then have the conversation. Finesse without a goal is just nice wording. See `principles/strategy-not-self-expression.md`.

## What finesse is NOT

- **Not softening the guarantees.** For a risk-minded reader (a CFO or security reviewer), explicit negatives such as "no journal entries" and "can't see the other entity" are the controls they asked for. Keep those as plain negatives, and run the negative-language audit on the hedges around them.
- **Not dodging a direct question.** When the reader asks "how many?", the answer has to be unambiguous, even when it's framed ("you'd be our first" answers "none" plainly).

- **Not lying or spin.** The facts stay true. You're choosing emphasis and framing, not inventing reality.
- **Not marketing-speak.** Person C is confident, not breathless. Over-framing ("blazing-fast, infinitely scalable, zero limits") destroys the credibility that finesse is meant to build.
- **Not Person B's hedging.** Caveating everything in the name of "honesty" isn't more honest, it just transfers your anxiety to the reader. State the caveat once, framed as a managed thing, then stop.

## Common failure mode

Two opposite failures:

1. **No framing (Person A):** stating the bare fact and letting the reader's worst-case imagination fill the vacuum.
2. **Over-framing or negative framing (Person B):** so many hedges that the true, good news reads as a warning.

The fix for both is the same: lead with the capability or the decision, state the constraint once as something handled, and make sure every word is defensible.

## Applying this

This bites hardest on **customer/marketing copy** and **managing up**. When a customer asks your company a capability or limits question, Person A and Person B answers are common and both leak trust. Run the negative-language audit on anything external before it goes out. When flagging a risk to the CEO, the same fact framed as "here's the risk and here's how it's handled" lands very differently from "this might be a problem." Finesse is the difference, and it's never dishonest, it's just judgment about which true sentence to send.
