# Speak with Accurate Confidence

**Source**: Wes Kao, interview on Lenny's Newsletter ("Become a better communicator")

## The core idea

Match your stated confidence to the evidence you actually have. Don't overstate hypotheses as facts. Don't understate strong recommendations.

Kao's point: most communication failures are confidence-calibration failures. Either the writer hedges a clear recommendation into mush, or asserts a guess as a certainty. Both erode trust over time.

## Why it matters

Audiences (especially execs) have finely-tuned BS detectors. They notice when "definitely 20%" turns out to be 12%. They notice when "I'm not sure but maybe possibly we should consider…" is followed by a strong, well-evidenced recommendation. Pattern-matching builds or destroys credibility quickly.

Accurate confidence is also kind. It tells the reader how much weight to put on what you're saying. Over-confidence forces them to do the calibration work themselves; under-confidence wastes their time.

## The four registers

| Confidence level | Language pattern | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| **Strong recommendation** | "Recommending X." / "We should X." / "Going with X." | You have evidence + thinking + you've considered alternatives |
| **Calibrated take** | "My read: X." / "Best guess: X, ~70% confidence." / "Leaning X." | You have a view but real uncertainty |
| **Open question** | "Open question: could be X or Y. Here's how I'd decide." | You genuinely don't know yet |
| **Information** | "FYI:" / "Sharing because…" | No view, just relevant data |

Pick the right register. Don't drift up (overstate) or down (hedge).

## Anti-patterns

### Over-hedging

- *"I think maybe we could possibly consider doing X"*: three hedges in seven words. Kills authority.
- *"I'm not sure, but…"* prefixed onto a perfectly evidence-backed statement.
- *"Just my opinion but…"* before a recommendation that took weeks of analysis.

These are usually self-protection, not honesty. They signal "don't blame me if I'm wrong", which the reader reads as "I'm not confident."

### Over-claiming

- *"Definitely 20% lift"* when you mean "best guess 20%, could be 10–30%."
- *"This will work"* about a hypothesis that hasn't been tested.
- *"Customers love it"* based on three positive comments.

These read as smart in the moment, then expensive when the number lands at 12%. Long-term, over-claiming destroys credibility faster than under-claiming.

## Calibrated language patterns

Kao's recommended pattern (paraphrased): *"Be confident about the recommendation; be honest about the uncertainty in the evidence."*

Some templates:

- *"Recommending X. Confidence is high on the direction; lower on the magnitude: best guess +15%, range +5–25%."*
- *"My read: X. Two things would change that: [a] [b]."*
- *"Going with X. Worth flagging: I'm extrapolating from a 3-customer sample, so the magnitude is rough."*
- *"Open question: could be X or Y. I'll have data in two weeks; happy to recommend then."*

These signal both *what to do* and *how much to weight it*. Senior readers love this: it removes work from them.

## Receipts before you credit

Accurate confidence isn't only about predictions and numbers. It applies just as hard to praise, credit, and characterisation. A compliment that's wrong costs more credibility than no compliment, and in feedback or a recommendation it can read as not having listened.

Before crediting someone (or a tool, or a result), check the specific against the source:

- **Don't credit work that didn't happen the way you're saying.** A draft credited AI for helping someone ramp on Mapbox; her own account was the opposite: AI kept getting Mapbox wrong, so she read the docs herself. Crediting it would have contradicted her and read as not-listening.
- **Don't inflate the scope of a claim.** "Tested with real users" when one client tested it once: "real users" implies the user base. Say what actually happened ("tested it offline from Launceston airport").
- **Get the domain facts right.** "Switched from Expo to React Native" reads as a framework swap to anyone technical, since Expo *is* React Native. Precision matters when the reader (or the subject) knows the space.

Rule of thumb: every specific, verifiable claim in a piece of praise or characterisation needs a receipt. If you can't point to where it came from, soften it or cut it.

This is the receipts check in the SKILL.md Step 6 self-check, and it pairs with the "no performative praise labels" rule in voice calibration: name the specific thing, and make sure the specific is true.

## Before / after

### Over-hedged

> I was thinking about it, and I'm not totally sure but I think maybe we could possibly consider switching to Adyen for payments? It might save us some money and the API seems a bit cleaner. Idk, what do you think?

Translation: "I have no view." But probably you do.

### Calibrated

> Recommending we switch to Adyen for payments. High confidence on the direction (~$120k/yr savings, cleaner API); medium confidence on the timing (eng has bandwidth in Q3 but Q2 would be tight).

Same idea, calibrated. Reader knows what to do and how confident you are.

### Over-claimed

> The new pricing model will lift ARR by 20%.

Translation: someone is going to hold you to 20%, and you don't actually know.

### Calibrated

> Best estimate is +20% ARR from the new pricing model. Range is roughly +10–30% based on benchmark data; we'll know within 30 days of launch.

## How this skill uses it

When refining drafts, watch for:

- Hedge stacking ("I think maybe we could perhaps consider") → strip to one calibrated hedge or a clean recommendation.
- Naked numbers stated as facts when they're estimates → add the range or confidence.
- "Just my thoughts" / "open to anything" softeners on strong takes → cut.
- "We will" / "this will" on hypotheses → soften to "expect to" / "modelling suggests".

Push toward the strongest *honest* version of each statement.

## Common failure mode

Confusing "humble" with "hedge-y." You can be a humble communicator while still stating recommendations clearly. Humility lives in the willingness to be proven wrong, not in linguistic softeners. *"Recommending X, happy to revisit if Y comes up"* is humble *and* clear. *"I think maybe X, idk"* is just unclear.
