# Audience: Peers / Teammates

**Strictness**: relaxed

## Who this is for

People you work with daily: other PMs, engineers on your team, designers you collaborate closely with, your pod. Lateral relationships where there's shared context, established trust, and frequent back-and-forth.

## Their reading context

- Already know most of the background.
- Not reading cold: they saw the Slack thread, remember yesterday's meeting.
- Tolerate casual tone; it's the norm.
- Want quick, actionable comms; long formal drafts feel weird.
- Value warmth and humour appropriately: it's part of the working relationship.

## Default principles to apply (in order)

1. **Concision**: still matters, just in a more relaxed register.
2. **Strategy not self-expression**: even in a casual Slack, know what you want.
3. **Clear ask**: peers appreciate not having to guess.
4. **Minimum Viable Backstory**: low, since context is shared.

Principles that apply **less** here:

- BLUF: nice, but casual openers are fine.
- Formal structure: usually unnecessary.
- MOO: if the peer will push back, that's welcome and normal; you don't always need to pre-empt.
- Managing up formality: irrelevant lateral.
- Finesse: low day-to-day (shared context and trust mean you can be plain), but it still matters for the sensitive lateral moments — declining, giving hard feedback, or a delicate ask. There, same facts / honest positive frame applies. See `principles/finesse.md`.

## What they want to see

- Plain, direct, warm communication.
- The ask / question / update, usually in the first message.
- Enough context to act, no more.
- Their voice being treated as welcome, not anticipated and pre-empted.

## What they don't want to see

- Formal structure on a casual Slack ("Dear Sam, I wanted to draft a brief overview…").
- Excessive hedging ("sorry to bother you again").
- Treating them like they don't remember yesterday.
- Corporate-speak.
- Long DMs when a 1-line message would do.

## Calibration vs. other audiences

| | CEO | Cross-func | Peer |
|---|---|---|---|
| BLUF | Mandatory | Yes | Usually |
| Greeting OK? | No | Sometimes | Yes ("hey!", "oi") |
| Warmth | Minimal | Moderate | Generous |
| Humour | Rare | OK | Welcome |
| Context depth | Minimum | Moderate | Low |
| Apology for taking time | No | No | No |

## Slack examples

**Good:**
> sam, is the billing webhook deployed to staging yet? need to test the retry case

Warm, direct, one ask. No pre-amble. No BLUF ceremony; the first word *is* the context.

**Good:**
> fyi i'm gonna push the release to tues, eng wants more QA time and i agree. yelling about it tomorrow in standup

Casual, informative, a touch of humour, clear action, implied next step (standup).

**Too formal (over-applying CEO rules to a peer):**
> Sam, Recommending we defer the release to Tuesday. Main risk: more QA cycles. Benefit: fewer rollbacks. Sign off?

Formal structure on a lateral Slack reads as cold / weird / performative. Don't.

**Too informal (under-applying strategy):**
> ugh do you even think the webhook thing is working? idk maybe just deploy and see

Too vague to act on. Peer-warm is fine; peer-useless is not.

## When peer comms *do* need strictness

- Handing off a task to someone who'll pick it up tomorrow (they need context, they weren't in the meeting)
- Writing a public channel message that others beyond the immediate peer will read
- Async comms across timezones (they won't ask follow-ups; front-load more)
- Anything that might get screenshotted or forwarded (treat it as one level more formal than you'd otherwise)

In these cases, borrow structure from the cross-functional playbook.

## Declining or saying no to a peer

When the message is a no (passing on a request, declining to pick something up, turning down an intro), keep it warm without overcommitting: carry the next step yourself, make any offer specific and bounded, and close on one genuine detail. See `principles/the-warm-no.md`.

## Strictness calibration

**Apply lightly:**
- BLUF: first sentence should still carry weight, but doesn't need to be formal
- Concision: yes, always, but don't strip warmth
- Ask clarity: always

**Skip:**
- Formal MOO
- Hero numbers
- Structured sign-offs
- Apology-stripping (peers already know you aren't excessively apologetic; minor filler is fine)

**Preserve:**
- Your voice (warm, dry, natural)
- Humour
- Casual openers ("oi", "hey", "quick one")

## What to push back on (when refining peer drafts)

- A peer Slack that reads like an exec memo → loosen it
- A peer message with no actual ask or purpose → tighten it or don't send
- Slack to a close peer that starts with a long preamble → cut the preamble
- Excessive formality that makes you sound like not-you → unwind

## Common failure mode

Two opposite failures:

1. **Over-applying formality** to peers because CEO habits leaked laterally. Makes you read as robotic.
2. **Under-applying strategy** to peers, treating every message as casual chat, leaving colleagues unsure what was being asked.

The fix: casual voice, strategic intent. Be warm, be short, be clear. It's possible to be all three.
