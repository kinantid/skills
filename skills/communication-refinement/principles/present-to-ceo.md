# Presenting to Your CEO: 12 Principles

**Source**: Wes Kao, "How to present to your CEO"

## The core idea

Presenting to a CEO (or any senior exec) isn't just a longer version of presenting to your manager. The audience, the stakes, and the cognitive budget are different. You need to adapt, not just content, but pacing, framing, and what you leave out.

(Note: this skill deliberately doesn't cover *slide decks*. These principles still apply to written CEO comms: memos, pre-reads, status updates, one-pagers, Slack DMs to the CEO.)

## The 12 principles

### 1. Know what decision you want
Every CEO interaction should have a named outcome. "Keep them informed" isn't an outcome. "Approve the Q3 hire", "Sign off on the launch date", "Bless the direction so I can go": those are outcomes. If you can't name the outcome, don't send the message.

### 2. Lead with the bottom line
BLUF. First sentence states the recommendation, decision, or ask. Not the background.

### 3. Respect their time absolutely
CEOs are compute-constrained. Every sentence must pass the "would they cut this?" test. Prune ruthlessly.

### 4. Anticipate their questions
Before sending, ask: "what are the 3 questions they'll have after reading?" Address them pre-emptively. This is MOO applied upward.

### 5. Know their priors
What do they already believe about this topic? What are they worried about this quarter? What did they last say about this area? Tune the message to where they actually are, not where you wish they were.

### 6. Give them the one number that matters
Data, yes, but one hero number. *"34% lift in activation"* lands harder than a table of 12 metrics. Put the hero number at the top. Details on request.

### 7. Be honest about what you don't know
CEOs see through false confidence fast. If you're uncertain, say so, calibrated. *"Best guess is 20%. High uncertainty; will tighten in two weeks."* That reads as strong; *"definitely 20%"* when you don't know reads as weak.

### 8. Don't ask them to do your job
Don't send a 10-option decision tree. Do the work: narrow to 2 options, recommend one, make the case, accept that they might override. That's their job; the narrowing is yours.

### 9. Skip the narrative arc
CEOs don't need the story. They need the conclusion. Save the journey for the post-mortem.

### 10. Make it scannable
Even a well-written paragraph gets skimmed. Use bold, headers, and signposts so that a skim still conveys the core.

### 11. Close with the next step
End every CEO comm with a concrete next action: by whom, by when. *"Will move forward Monday unless I hear otherwise"* beats *"let me know your thoughts"* every time.

### 12. Accept the speed of exec feedback
CEOs respond in 20 words or not at all. Don't over-read tone or ambiguity. If you got a thumbs up, you got a yes. If you got silence, follow up once after 24 hrs; if still silence, assume implicit delegation and move.

## Quick application checklist

Before sending anything to a CEO, verify:

- [ ] First sentence states the point / ask / recommendation.
- [ ] One hero number or fact is highlighted.
- [ ] Three most obvious questions are answered in the body.
- [ ] Message is scannable (bold, bullets, or structure).
- [ ] Closing sentence specifies next action and owner.
- [ ] Uncertainty is calibrated, not hidden.
- [ ] No hedging ("just", "maybe", "wondering if").
- [ ] Can it be cut by 30%? Cut it.

## Before / after

**Before (2 paragraphs of setup before the ask):**
> Hi [CEO], hope your week's going well. I wanted to loop you in on where we've landed with the onboarding work. As you know, we've been exploring a few directions and getting input from eng, design and customer success. It's been a productive process and we've learned a lot. I think we're now at the point where we need to make a call on which direction to go. The two main options are: (a) an in-app tour approach, which the design team prefers, or (b) a checklist-based flow, which has more data behind it. Both have pros and cons but after weighing everything I'm leaning towards (b). Let me know your thoughts when you have a chance.

**After (BLUF):**
> [CEO], going with the checklist onboarding flow (option b). Data is stronger; design prefers option a but the ARR lift from b is 3x larger based on the prototype.
>
> Two risks: (1) build is 2 weeks longer; (2) requires minor backend change. Both scoped with eng, no blockers.
>
> Shipping Monday unless you want to chat.

First version: 130+ words of setup, ask at the end, no hero number.
Second version: 55 words, hero number up top (*"3x larger"*), MOO addressed (risks + why they're handled), clear close (*"Monday unless you want to chat"*).

## Applying this

When writing to your CEO, default to this treatment unless there's a relationship-specific reason to soften. Internal CEO comms in Slack can be slightly less structured than formal memos, but BLUF and hero number should hold even in a one-line Slack.

## Common failure mode

Trying to show the work. New-ish PMs often want the CEO to see how much thinking went into the recommendation. Resist. The CEO isn't grading your effort. They're making a decision. If they want the working, they'll ask. Keep the primary message tight, offer more detail on request.
