# Strategy, Not Self-Expression

**Source**: Wes Kao, "Strategy, not self-expression"

## The core idea

When you write for work, your job isn't to express yourself, it's to move the reader. Those are different goals, and they produce different drafts.

Self-expression asks: "what do I want to say?"
Strategy asks: "what does the reader need in order to do / decide / feel what I want them to do / decide / feel?"

The second mindset produces communication that lands. The first produces communication that's authentic but ineffective: the writer feels heard, the reader remains unmoved.

## Why it matters

Most bad professional writing is actually good self-expression. It's a faithful record of the writer's thinking process: every consideration, every caveat, every qualifier. But the reader doesn't care about your process. They care about their decision.

Kao's framing: treat every piece of writing as a small act of persuasion with a specific desired outcome. If you don't know what outcome you want, you're venting, not communicating.

## How to apply it

Before writing anything that matters, answer:

1. **Who is reading this?** (Specifically. Not "the team." Which person, with what priors?)
2. **What do I want them to do / decide / feel after reading?** (Specifically. Not "understand the situation." Approve X? Unblock Y? Feel reassured?)
3. **What's between them and that outcome right now?** (Objections? Missing context? Competing priorities?)

Then write to close that gap. Nothing else belongs in the draft.

## The test

After drafting, ask: "Does every sentence push the reader toward the outcome I want?"

Kill anything that doesn't. Even if it's true. Even if it's interesting. Even if you like it.

Especially if you like it. That's often the sign that you wrote it for you, not for them.

## Self-expression signals to watch for

- **Over-explaining your process.** *"I spent the weekend thinking about this and went back and forth a few times before landing on…"* The reader doesn't need your journey; they need your conclusion.
- **Venting framed as update.** *"It's been a frustrating week because marketing hasn't been responsive…"* If it's not actionable, it's venting.
- **Clever openings.** Puns, analogies, quotes that show you're witty but don't advance the point.
- **Long qualifications.** *"Of course, there are many ways to look at this, and I don't want to oversimplify, but…"* Hedging as a form of self-protection, not clarity.
- **Emotional signaling.** *"I'm really excited/worried/passionate about…"* Your emotions aren't what the reader needs to know; the implication is.

## Before / after

**Before (self-expression):**
> I've been thinking a lot lately about our customer feedback loop. I genuinely care about our users and I've always felt that we could be doing more to really hear them out. After a lot of reflection and reading some great articles on the topic (happy to share), I've come to the view that we should probably consider setting up a more formal system. I know change is hard but I think this is important. Open to thoughts.

**After (strategy):**
> Proposing a monthly customer advisory call: 8 users, 1 hour, structured agenda. Three reasons:
>
> 1. Current feedback is ad hoc; we're missing signal on usage patterns.
> 2. Top 3 competitors run this; enterprise buyers ask about it.
> 3. Low cost: I can run it, ~3 hours/month.
>
> Want to start in May. Approve?

The first version is about the writer's journey. The second is about the reader's decision. Same topic; completely different communication.

## Applying this

This principle is especially useful when drafting internally about a topic you care about personally. Passion and strategy aren't in conflict, but they're different drafts. Write the passionate one in your notes; send the strategic one to the CEO.

## Common failure mode

Confusing "direct" with "cold." Strategic communication doesn't have to be robotic. You can still be warm, funny, human, just make sure the warmth serves the strategy. A well-placed piece of warmth can *be* the strategy (e.g., rebuilding trust with a frustrated customer). Self-expression is when the warmth is there for you, not for them.

## When self-expression is the strategy

In some contexts (condolence notes, apology messages, relationship-building DMs, personal thought-leadership writing), authentic self-expression *is* the strategic move. The question isn't "be strategic or be authentic", it's "is being authentic the right move for this outcome?" Often yes. Sometimes no. Name the goal first; the voice follows.
