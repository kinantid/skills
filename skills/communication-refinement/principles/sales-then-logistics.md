# Sales, Then Logistics

**Source**: Wes Kao, interview on Lenny's Newsletter ("Become a better communicator")

## The core idea

Before you tell people *how* to do something (logistics), tell them *why it matters* (sales). Even (especially) when they seem busy or senior.

Kao's framing: people skip the sales because they assume the audience already gets the *why*. They don't, or they need a reminder, or their attention is elsewhere. Without the *why*, the *how* doesn't land.

## Why it matters

Most professional communication front-loads logistics: dates, numbers, steps, asks. The reader processes the surface information but doesn't internalise *the point*, so they don't prioritise the work, don't push back productively, don't advocate for the idea later.

30–60 seconds of "sales" before the logistics is enough to anchor the reader. Skip it and the rest of the message gets discounted.

## How to apply it

Before any meaningful ask or update, build a tiny "sales" preamble:

1. **What's the stake?** What changes if this works (or doesn't)?
2. **Why now?** What's the trigger that makes this urgent or relevant *today*?
3. **What's the upside or risk?** A specific, named outcome, not "improvement", but the actual thing.

Then go into logistics: dates, decisions, steps, asks.

Length: usually 1–3 sentences. Sometimes one well-chosen sentence is enough. The point isn't a sermon, it's an anchor.

## Sales-then-logistics, by audience

| Audience | Sales sentence sounds like |
|---|---|
| **CEO** | A single line tying the ask to a Q-level outcome or strategic risk |
| **Cross-functional lead** | A line connecting your ask to *their* function's metric / pain |
| **Peer** | Often skippable if shared context is strong; otherwise one line on the pain |
| **Customer** | The specific outcome / pain relief: usually the lead, not the preamble |

## Before / after

### Without sales (logistics-only)

> [CEO], pushing the launch from 13 May to 20 May. Eng needs another week for the billing integration. Will brief sales today.

This is a 3-sentence message that gives your CEO no reason to care. They'll register the date change but won't process whether to push back, accelerate, or escalate.

### With sales

> [CEO], pushing the launch from 13 May to 20 May, to protect the [customer] deal ($180k, billing-dependent). Eng needs another week to harden the billing integration; without it, we risk shipping with a bug [customer] would hit on day one. Briefing sales today.

Same logistics, but the *why* is anchored ("protect the [customer] deal", "ship with a bug [customer] would hit"). Now your CEO knows what's at stake and can engage with the decision rather than just acknowledging it.

## When sales is the lead

For a high-stakes pitch or recommendation, the sales sentence often *is* the BLUF: the most important line. The "logistics" follows underneath as proof / mechanism.

Example (sales as lead):
> Recommending we move to Adyen: saves $120k/yr in fees and unblocks the international expansion roadmap. Migration is 4 weeks, no customer impact, rollback via config flag.

The first sentence sells. The second handles logistics + MOO. Whole thing is 30 words.

## Common failure mode

Two opposites:

1. **Skipping sales entirely.** Most common in updates. Reader registers the facts, doesn't engage with the meaning.
2. **Padding sales into a sermon.** Three paragraphs of *why this matters* before the actual ask. Reader gets impatient and tunes out.

Aim for a *short, specific* sales sentence. If it takes more than 3 sentences, your sales is doing too much work. Either the ask doesn't really matter, or you haven't found the actual stake.

## How this skill uses it

When refining anything that's a pitch, recommendation, or update with strategic stakes, check: does the message tell the reader *why this matters* before getting into mechanics? If no, add one sales sentence. If the sales sentence runs over three lines, compress.

Especially for upward and customer-facing comms: those are the audiences most likely to discount logistics-only messages.
