# CEDAF: The Delegation Framework

**Source**: Wes Kao, interview on Lenny's Newsletter ("Become a better communicator")

## The core idea

When you're delegating work (to a teammate, a cross-functional partner, an agency, even an AI), most failures aren't because the person can't do the work. They're because the handoff was incomplete.

Kao's CEDAF framework names the five dimensions a complete handoff has to cover:

1. **C**omprehension: they understand what needs to be done
2. **E**xcitement: the task feels meaningful and motivating
3. **D**e-risk: predictable failure modes are anticipated and addressed
4. **A**lign: both sides agree on scope, success, and ownership
5. **F**eedback: the loop for course-correction is set up before work starts

Skip any one and the work degrades.

## Why it matters for written communication

CEDAF isn't just for verbal management. It shows up constantly in writing. Every time you:

- Briefs an engineer or designer in writing
- Asks a cross-functional lead to take something on
- Hands a task to a contractor or agency
- Sets up a customer handoff between teams
- Writes a spec or PRD

…the same five dimensions need to land. CEDAF gives you a checklist for whether the writing is complete enough to actually unblock the work.

## How to apply it

Before sending a delegation message, run through:

### 1. Comprehension: do they understand the task?
- Is the goal stated explicitly? Not "let's improve onboarding" but "raise activation from 28% to 38%."
- Is the *deliverable* specified? Not "look into pricing" but "by Thursday: a 1-pager comparing tiered vs. usage with a recommendation."
- Are key terms / context clear?

### 2. Excitement: is the meaning clear?
- Does the message explain *why* this matters? (See: sales-then-logistics)
- Does the framing connect to something the person cares about?
- Is the energy of the message matched to the importance of the work?

Excitement isn't fake enthusiasm. It's making sure the meaning is there. A bored brief produces bored work.

### 3. De-risk: are the predictable failure modes addressed?
- What's the *most likely* way this gets stuck or goes wrong?
- Have you flagged the constraints they need to know? (Budget? Stakeholder X? A known landmine?)
- Is there a fallback if the main approach hits a wall?

### 4. Align: is there shared agreement on what done looks like?
- Has the person had a chance to push back / clarify scope?
- Is success defined in measurable / observable terms?
- Is ownership clear: who decides, who does, who reviews?

### 5. Feedback: is the loop set up?
- When's the next check-in?
- What's the lightest possible signal that things are off-track?
- How will you know if you should re-scope?

## Structural template (delegation message)

```
[The ask, in one line: comprehension]

[Why it matters, in one line: excitement]

[The constraint or risk worth flagging: de-risk]

[Success looks like X by Y: alignment]

[Check-in / feedback rhythm: feedback]
```

## Example: delegating a research task

### Without CEDAF

> Hey Sam, can you look into our pricing options and let me know what you think? Thanks!

Fails on most dimensions. No clear deliverable, no why, no constraints, no alignment, no feedback rhythm. Sam will either over-deliver (waste effort) or under-deliver (miss the mark).

### With CEDAF

> Sam, want a 1-pager by Thursday recommending tiered vs. usage pricing for mid-market. **(C)**
>
> This unlocks the Q3 ARR target. We've been blocked on a recommendation for two weeks and it's the last thing in the way. **(E)**
>
> Watch out for: existing customers can't be repriced (legal constraint); CFO will push back on anything that risks current MRR. **(D)**
>
> "Done" = pick one, two-paragraph rationale, one risk + mitigation. **(A)**
>
> Quick check-in Wed afternoon if it'd help; otherwise just send the doc Thursday morning. **(F)**

Now Sam knows: what to produce, why it matters, what to avoid, what done looks like, and how to course-correct.

## When CEDAF can be lighter

Not every delegation needs the full template. Lighter versions for:

- **Recurring tasks**: comprehension and alignment are already shared; just say "do the usual."
- **Trusted senior teammates**: they fill in gaps from context; you can lean on a 1-line brief.
- **Tiny tasks**: "can you reply to that customer?" doesn't need a feedback loop.

But for anything that's:
- New
- Cross-functional
- High-stakes
- Going to take more than a few hours
- Going to a person who'll work async

…run the full CEDAF check.

## How this skill uses it

When refining a delegation message (to a teammate, a contractor, a cross-functional partner), run a quick CEDAF audit. If any dimension is missing, name what's missing and suggest the fix.

Common failure patterns:

- Excitement missing → message is dry, sounds like assigning chores. Add the *why*.
- De-risk missing → known landmine isn't flagged. The reader will hit it and bounce back to you for clarification.
- Alignment missing → "what does done look like?" is left implicit. The output won't match what you wanted.
- Feedback missing → no check-in rhythm. Either you'll micromanage or they'll go silent for a week.

## Common failure mode

CEDAF as bureaucracy. The framework isn't a meeting agenda or a 5-section template you have to fill out. It's a *check*: five dimensions to make sure aren't missing. A single well-written paragraph can hit all five if it's tight enough.

The test: after writing the brief, pretend you're the recipient. Could you act on it tomorrow without coming back to ask anything? If yes, CEDAF's covered. If no, find which dimension is missing.
